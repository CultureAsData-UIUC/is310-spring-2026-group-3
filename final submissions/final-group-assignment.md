## Rohan:

We thought when we started this project that working with music data would be simple- just get songs, categorize them and look for patterns. Instead, we found it to be much more difficult to work with music data than we had ever imagined. The first major lesson we learned is that data is created, not just collected. When labeling our music data, we ourselves had to create the labels that we were using, and we didn't always agree on the definitions of those categories. We found that because cultural data will always be subjective, we didn't need to "fix" that, but instead just be explicit about how the categories were defined. We also found that music is so much richer and more complex than it can possibly be rendered into data; a single song could have a happy, nostalgic and peaceful feel all at once, but when placed in a dataset, you can only choose one label and simplify the experience to lose any sort of meaning. We also noticed that platforms like Spotify influence the music we listen to in huge ways. Although we were choosing our own music, many songs that we chose are found in curated lists, or have been placed there by algorithms, and so reflect a personal preference but one which is heavily swayed by external factors. I, in particular, had to decide to track my listening behavior and build a manual data set. I gathered 75 songs and the labels; time of day, mood, and energy, as well as a 'skipped' variable. Through doing this I was able to gain a real appreciation of how difficult it can be to codify subjective experience into a label. I then also integrated our dataset with an API, adding more playlist data and using that data to scale up the dataset even further. It was at this point I realized that scaling, while necessary for consistent data, removed much of the intimacy of the dataset. The scaling aspect changed the way we had to interact with the data. For us to develop principles to continue our work we focused on defining the scope of our problem, accepting that the data can be subjective, defining categories consistently, realizing the influence of platforms, and being transparent with our limitations. Working collaboratively on data collection, scaling, cleaning and the writing was essential, as this gave us the ability to see the data from all angles.

The biggest thing that we have learned about this project has been how much interpretation is involved in working with music as data.

## Inika:

One of the biggest lessons we learned from this project is that the music datasets provided by streaming sites don’t always necessarily present a neutral view of musical culture. Initially, one would think that numbers like danceability, valence, popularity, and energy were objective indicators of the songs' qualities. However, after looking deeper into the Spotify datasets, we realized that these metrics really just represent computationally done statistics of proprietary systems and should not be looked at as neutral representations of songs. Therefore, we recommend that future researchers should invest in learning more about what each metric represents and what musical experience they cannot convey in a dataset.

Another important lesson we learned is how popularity really is more of a concept that has been constructed. Although streaming datasets usually represent popularity through numerical data such as streams or chart rankings, this popularity value was actually a product of multiple things including playlisting, recommendation algorithms, marketing systems, and visibility afforded by the platform. In most cases, it turns out that streaming platforms are not just measuring listeners’ preferences. The platforms actually play a big role behind the scenes in what large groups of people get exposed to.

Another thing we discovered is that computational classification methods usually oversimplify emotions in such a complex topic like music. There might often be multiple conflicting types of emotional elements in songs, like happy backing tracks but sad lyrics. When this happens, datasets usually tend to collapse them into numbers or just one mood.

The first thing I would give advice on to future researchers is that musical data has to be treated as a complex system. If it is simplified, we lose a lot of the nuance that comes with music. Music data is created within platforms, recommendation algorithms, and businesses, all at the same time. We need to account for all of these factors.

In my role in the project, my emphasis was mainly on auditing metrics coming from Spotify. This meant generating categories of interpretative moods, popularity, and visibility in order to see how popularity and mood are classified computationally by streaming sites.

## Beau

I initially focused my project on how computationally generated data could actively support expanding human culture instead of merely reproducing it. In this case, the impetus was to analyze how Spotify's algorithmic classification system can help facilitate recommendations and predict new genres to enhance users' musical experiences. As I explored the algorithms, I encountered a major structural tension around the way streaming platforms treat "genres" as rigid, fixed boundaries for marketing purposes versus being fluid forms of artistic expression. If an algorithm is only built to use existing flat tags to recommend music, it continues to limit users' musical tastes.

To address this issue, I created a 75 song dataset that showed a defined trajectory through Hip-Hop, R&B, and Pop using an Audit and Augment strategy. As I examined this dataset, it became apparent to me that when using computational methods to expand users' musical tastes, you cannot only rely on numeric features that are proprietary. Instead, I needed to manually augment my dataset with custom qualitative categories known as Human_Vibe_Tags and Discovery_Reasons. The qualitative interpretive layer between the three genres served to illustrate that a high-energy Hip-Hop song can be a logical acoustic bridge to an uptempo Pop song.

## Eshaan:

My piece of this project started off asking why a song gets added to a playlist, but it ended up somewhere different. After dealing with Spotify's unbearable Web API I switched the question into something the available data could actually answer. Which of 2023's most streamed songs survived into the 2025 Wrapped and the all time streamed lists. Less than half of them did. 47 out of 100. That changed the way I was thinking about popularity. A song with two billion streams in 2023 still could randomly either be culturally relevant three years later or disappear, and the audio features that streaming platforms use to describe songs such as valence and energy can barely predict where these songs will end up. My regression model explained only about a quarter of the variance in persistence. The rest sits outside the audio data entirely. It lives in social context, in TikTok and in the algorithmic exposure that the platform itself shapes, and in things the dataset just can't see and show us in numbers.

The biggest lesson I learned and would probably pass down is that the question you start with isn't always the question the data lets you answer. I went through three versions of the research question before landing on longevity, and each pivot was forced by some random constraint in what I could actually pull. That's just the normal shape of data work when your data comes from a platform that controls what you can see.

That's the other lesson, and honestly the one that surprised me most. Spotify's API access tiering means a researcher creating a new developer app in 2026 cannot pull the popularity field, audio features, artist genres, follower counts, or full playlist tracklists for arbitrary public playlists. For a student trying to do music as data it's really annoying what you are limited to when most of this data is available through their app anyways. What we end up calling "cultural data" is shaped at the access layer first. Platforms decide who gets to build bigger datasets, and that decision shapes everything that comes after. 

My contribution to the project was looking at what keeps a song culturally relevant once its moment passes. That meant pulling together datasets from different time periods to see which songs from 2023 actually made it to the top 100 songs 2 - 3 years from then, and taking a deeper look into the audio features to figure out what told the persistent ones apart from the songs that fizzled out of the popular eye.

A few principles:

Plan for a pivot. The first research question you ask is a hypothesis about what you think can be done. Treat it that way and be ready to revise once you find out what can and cannot be done. You will have to revise and revise and revise. But that is part of the process.

Use cross period comparisons where you can. A single timeframe of a popularity dataset is a single moment that can obscure what can be pulled from long term changes and trends. By expanding you’ll open up the door to more meaningful information that can be backed by time. 

## Raymond:

Another big challenge for music, culture, and new methods of computation is the advancement of generative AI, most notably the types capable of creating media like video or audio. Every day, these AIs get better and better at doing their one task, which is to create something that humans believe is human-made. While advanced benchmarks are used for evaluation of text based LLMs, it’s difficult to assign a definitive score to how “well” an AI generated an audio. At the end of the day, it all boils back down to being a really complicated and expensive Turing test for whether the AI results sound/look like human results, or like AI results. 

My project audited an existing Kaggle dataset of 114,000 Spotify tracks to produce a curated corpus of approximately 52,000 "underground" songs defined as tracks with a popularity score between 5 and 60. Rather than collecting new data, the project applied three sequential computational filters (popularity threshold, English-language genre proxy, and deduplication) using pandas, then performed exploratory analysis of audio features including danceability, energy, valence, and tempo. The goal was to study songs that have real listener bases but have not broken into mainstream popularity. 

The most significant struggle was discovering that the concept I actually wanted to study, which was "dark horse" songs that were culturally underrated despite charting, had no reliable computational signal. I had no chart data, couldn't personally evaluate thousands of unfamiliar songs, and had no way to automate that judgment. The pivot to a popularity-based definition of "underground" was a direct lesson in letting computational constraints shape the research question. Beyond the pivot, working closely with Spotify's audio features revealed a persistent gap between what data measures and what it means: high danceability and energy scores do not tell you anything about a song's subject matter or emotional intent. The data captures the sound of music; it does not capture the experience of it. That limitation is the central thing I would want any future researcher using platform music data to understand before they start. 

## Guidelines for Future Researchers' Methodologies:

Look for Edges, Not Clusters: Real cultural examination and discovery of sound and music within the constraints of the different category boundaries will only happen outside of them; a song does not belong entirely within a box just because some data has been entered as such.

Balance Automation with Context: Most algorithmic representation of auditory data will typically eliminate the culturally linked aspects of that data, by building human-injected interpretation based on aspects such as feeling and transitions into the data structure, future researchers will be able to create recommendation systems that feel like a natural fit rather than forced.

Expect Genre Creep with Scale: When creating small amounts of data, you will have the ability to hear and understand the many subtle differences in genres, however, when you scale your data creation through computational means, you will lose that human "ear," therefore a very strict set of pattern-matching rules will need to be established so that the recommendations will neither be completely random nor too safe.
